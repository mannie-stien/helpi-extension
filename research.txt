SECTION 5: EDGE CASES
Multiple poultry houses:

Core schema must be recorded per house, not per farm
Minimum addition: a house identifier (House A / House B / "big shed" / "small shed")
One extra field, written once per entry: low burden
Critical because theft or overfeeding in one house is masked by correct usage in another if combined
Unknown: how many small-medium Ghana farms have 2+ separate feed stores vs. one central store — this determines whether per-house tracking is necessary or optional

Farm size differences:

Small farms (300-500 birds): likely measuring in scoops not bags; bags last multiple days; adjust frequency to "bags opened this week" rather than daily if daily is too coarse
Medium farms (1,500-5,000 birds): bags consumed daily; daily recording natural and meaningful
Schema itself doesn't change; only the unit and frequency of the number field

Layers vs. broilers:

Broilers: consumption increases with age (day 1 birds eat far less than week 5 birds); comparison must be age-normalized
Layers: consumption is relatively stable per bird once laying begins; simpler to track
Schema is identical for both; interpretation of anomalies differs
For broilers: week-over-week comparison is more meaningful than day-over-day because age-driven growth distorts daily comparison
Unknown: whether Ghanaian small-layer farmers track feed differently from broiler farmers in practice — needs field verification

Shared responsibility between owner and farmhand:

Farmhand most likely to record daily usage (they handle the feed)
Owner most likely to record deliveries (they purchase and receive)
Risk: farmhand who is stealing will under-record usage to mask the gap
Mitigation: owner should periodically count physical bags on hand and compare to expected inventory; this cross-check requires owner involvement at least weekly
Schema must specify that owner independently verifies inventory count once per week — not daily, not complex, just a physical count
This is a behavior requirement, not a data field

Farmer literacy and numeracy edge case:

If farmer cannot calculate daily average mentally, the consumption comparison signal fails
Minimum viable numeracy required: subtraction and rough division
If below this threshold, schema still works for simple "do I have fewer bags than expected?" check
Pattern comparison signals may need external help (trusted person, service agent) to interpret

Multiple poultry houses:

If each house has different batch start dates, treat each house as a separate batch with its own batch ID
Price data is farm-level (market price is same regardless of house)
No additional fields needed; just disciplined use of batch ID

Farm size differences:

Small broiler farm (300 birds): batch cycles every 6-8 weeks; low frequency of sell events
Medium broiler farm (2,000+ birds): may run 2-3 batches simultaneously; batch IDs become critical

Layers vs. broilers:

Different schemas needed because different decision cycles

Connectivity edge case:

WhatsApp price announcements — farmer writes it down, doesn't need to type into any system
No connectivity dependency in this schema

SECTION 5: EDGE CASES
Multiple suppliers (common on medium farms):

Schema is designed for this: supplier name field separates all records
Farmer maintains one running ledger but entries are tagged by supplier
Practical constraint: if farmer has 5+ suppliers, weekly balance review takes longer
Unknown: how many concurrent suppliers does the average Ghanaian small-medium poultry farm maintain? Field research needed to size the review burden

Supplier also provides credit for non-feed items (medication, chicks):

Same schema applies: just record the supplier name, amount, and type
No additional field needed — schema is category-agnostic
This is a feature of minimalism: the schema doesn't care what was purchased

Partial payments (very common):

Schema handles this naturally: one (+) entry for purchase, multiple (−) entries as payments are made
Balance recalculates correctly with each entry
Farmer must be consistent: every partial payment gets its own (−) entry on the date it was made

Spouse or worker making payments on behalf of owner:

High risk: spouse sends MoMo to supplier but farmer doesn't record it
Leads to "I think my wife paid... I'm not sure" scenario
Behavioral rule required, not a data field: whoever makes payment must notify owner immediately; owner records it
Alternative: designate owner as sole payment recorder; spouse must always report payments same day
Unknown: what percentage of small farm payment errors come from this specific scenario vs. farmer's own poor recording — needs investigation

Cash purchases (no credit created):

Field 5 (credit or cash) handles this: cash = no balance entry required
However: cash purchases should still be recorded for cost tracking purposes
The (+) notation for cash is: purchase happened, paid same day, balance = zero immediately
Temptation to skip recording cash purchases is high — this is a behavioral risk, not a schema gap

Pre-existing disputed balances:

Farmer and supplier already disagree on balance before schema begins
Schema cannot retroactively resolve old disputes
Starting point requires farmer and supplier to agree on opening balance
Unknown: how many farmers would have this obstacle when starting fresh record-keeping — could be significant adoption barrier
Practical approach: record "opening balance agreed on [date]: GH₵X,000 owed to [Supplier]" as first entry

Supplier using their own book (parallel tracking):

Schema is entirely on farmer's side; supplier keeps their own records
Goal is not to replace supplier records but to give farmer equal information power
In dispute: farmer shows their record; supplier shows theirs; they reconcile
Schema gives farmer a defensible position — currently they have none

SECTION 5: EDGE CASES
Multiple poultry houses:

If different houses have different batches or different suppliers, mortality must be recorded per house
Add one field: House ID (House A / House B, or "big shed")
Without it, high mortality in one house is masked by healthy birds in another
This is the only scenario requiring an additional field beyond the core 6

Multiple batches running simultaneously (medium farms):

Batch ID field handles this: each house-batch combination gets its own ID
Daily recording: farmer writes death count per batch ID — slightly more complex but manageable
"Batch 7-HouseA: 3 dead. Batch 8-HouseB: 1 dead." = 30 seconds

Farm size differences:

Small farm (300-500 birds): low absolute death numbers; 0-2 deaths/day typical; recording is trivial
Medium farm (2,000-5,000 birds): higher absolute numbers; patterns emerge faster (more data); slightly more likely to notice patterns
Schema identical for both; pattern detection timeline faster for medium farms due to larger sample sizes

Layers vs. broilers:

Broilers: 6-8 week cycle, high importance of age-week patterns
Layers: 72-96 week cycle, mortality patterns matter but are slower to develop
For layers, recording deaths weekly rather than daily is sufficient and reduces burden
Supplier comparison still valid for layers: point-of-lay pullet quality varies by supplier
Same schema works for both; only recording frequency differs

Farmhand reports deaths:

Worker collects dead birds every morning (this already happens everywhere)
Worker tells farmer the number
Farmer writes it down — not worker — to preserve data integrity
Risk: farmhand reports wrong number (either innocent error or concealing mortality)
Mitigation: farmer should physically see dead birds periodically (weekly) to cross-check
This is a behavioral check, not a data field

Worker forgets to remove dead birds from pen:

If dead birds accumulate and are removed in batches, daily count becomes inaccurate
Behavioral requirement: daily dead-bird removal must be a routine task
Unknown: how consistently this practice is maintained across small-medium Ghana farms

Incomplete batch data (farmer misses several days):

If farmer misses 3 days of recording due to travel, illness, or emergency, batch total becomes an undercount
Options: estimate missing days based on surrounding days' rates; note gap in record
Pattern detection tolerates some data gaps as long as batch totals are roughly accurate
Perfectionism about data completeness would discourage farmers from continuing after a missed stretch

Pre-existing records (farmer has been farming for years):

Farmer may remember roughly: "That March batch from Akosua was bad, maybe 15% loss"
These historical memories can be written as estimated batch summaries to serve as baseline
No back-filling of daily records needed — only batch-level estimates useful for pattern priming
This lowers the cold-start problem: farmer doesn't need 6 months of new data; has informal history to start from

CROSS-SCHEMA OBSERVATIONS
1. Overlap and integration opportunities:

Batch start date appears in Pain #2 and Pain #5: one entry serves both
Flock count appears in Pain #1 and Pain #5: one entry serves both
A unified "batch page" at batch start could capture: batch ID, start date, supplier, starting count, target sell date — all 5 fields in one 2-minute setup event

2. The 20-second-per-day ceiling:

Pains #3 and #4 have zero daily recording burden on days with no transactions
Pains #1 and #5 require one number per day
Pain #2 (broilers) requires near-zero daily effort
Total daily burden across all 5 pains: under 3 minutes per day on heaviest days

3. The delayed value problem:

Pains #1, #3, #4: value visible within days to weeks
Pain #2: value visible within 1-2 batch cycles (6-14 weeks)
Pain #5: value visible only after 4-6 batches (5-8 months minimum)
Implication: Pains #1, #3, and #4 must generate early wins that motivate continued recording for Pain #5's delayed payoff

4. The single biggest schema risk:

All five schemas assume farmer will consistently record one number at one consistent time each day
Behavior consistency — not data complexity — is the primary implementation risk
The schemas are minimal enough to succeed; the habit formation around them is the real design challenge
